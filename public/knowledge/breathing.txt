## Wim Hof Breathing

---

# OVERVIEW

Wim Hof Breathing is a controlled breathing method developed by Wim Hof. It combines rhythmic deep breathing, breath retention after exhalation, and a short recovery breath hold. The technique is commonly practiced for relaxation, mental focus, stress resilience, and increased body awareness.

HarmonyBreath presents this breathing method for educational and wellness purposes only. It does not provide medical advice and should not replace professional healthcare.

---

# HOW A SESSION WORKS

A complete session consists of multiple rounds.

Each round follows this sequence:

### Phase 1 – Guided Breathing

* Take deep, controlled inhales.
* Relax the exhale naturally.
* Continue for **30 breaths**.
* Total guided breathing time is **90 seconds**.

---

### Phase 2 – Breath-Out Hold

* After the final inhale, gently exhale and hold the breath.
* Stay relaxed throughout the hold.
* Never force the hold beyond a comfortable limit.
* Once the target hold duration is reached, the system automatically sounds a chime and transitions to Phase 3.

---

### Phase 3 – Recovery Breath

* Take one deep inhale.
* Hold the inhale for **15 seconds**.
* Exhale naturally.
* Begin the next round.

## Inhale

* Deep but comfortable.
* Breathe in deeply in whatever way feels most comfortable and natural to you.

## Exhale

* Relax naturally.
* Do not force all the air out.
* Maintain a smooth, slow rhythm (~2.5s inhale / ~2.5s exhale).

The breathing should always feel relaxed rather than rushed.

* While driving
* While cycling
* While swimming
* During free diving
* While operating machinery
* In any place where losing consciousness could be dangerous

* Sitting comfortably
* Or lying down
* In a safe environment

People who are pregnant or have significant medical conditions should consult a qualified healthcare professional before practicing.

BOX BREATHING STARTS FROM here 

Box breathing, also referred to as square breathing, is a deep breathing technique that can help you slow down your breathing. It works by distracting your mind as you count to four, calming your nervous system, and decreasing stress in your body.

Box breathing is a simple but powerful relaxation technique that can help return your breathing pattern to a relaxed rhythm. It can clear and calm your mind, improving your focus.

Getting Started With Box Breathing
When doing box breathing, it’s good to:

Sit in a chair, stand, or lie down on your back with one hand on your chest and one hand on your stomach. When you sit on a chair, ensure that your back is supported and your feet are firmly on the floor.
Breathe as you would normally for a minute.
Observe the rise and fall of your chest and stomach.
If you notice that your chest is rising but your stomach is not, you are shallow breathing. If your stomach is rising, you are deep breathing, activating full relaxation in your body.
Be aware of your breath to ensure that you are taking deep breaths, allowing your stomach to rise.
If you are lying down or seated on a chair, you will feel your back pressed against the surface when you take a deep breath.
If this is your first time practicing box breathing, push your stomach out while focusing on smooth, deep breaths.
Practice this regularly to encourage deep breathing on a daily basis.

How to do Box Breathing
Box breathing is easy and quick to learn. Anyone can practice this technique and it’s useful in stressful situations when you want to re-center yourself or improve concentration.

Four Steps to Master Box Breathing

Step 1: Breathe in, counting to four slowly. Feel the air enter your lungs.
Step 2: Hold your breath for 4 seconds. Try to avoid inhaling or exhaling for 4 seconds.
Step 3: Slowly exhale through your mouth for 4 seconds.
Step 4: Wait for another 4 seconds before you breathe in again. 
Repeat this exercise until you feel re-centered. Thirty seconds of deep breathing will help you feel more relaxed and in control.

Progression Rules

* All four phases should always remain equal in duration.
* Increase all phases together when progressing.
* Never increase only one phase.
* Users should only move to a longer duration once they can comfortably complete the previous level.
* The technique should always remain calm, smooth, and relaxed.

Custom Session

Allow users to customize:

duration from 2 sec to 10 sec for all phase

The application should validate all custom values and prevent unrealistic or unsafe durations.

Benefits of Box Breathing
Box breathing can be useful in many situations such as:

It can help you cope with panic and stress when feeling overwhelmed. Counting helps to take the focus from the panic-producing situation enabling you to handle and control your response.
Helps you to sleep when you are having insomnia.
Helps to control hyperventilation as you can instruct your lungs to breathe rhythmically.
Helps you refocus when you are having a busy or stressful day.
Eases panic and worry.
Keeps you calm while preparing for the day.
When making a big decision.
Lowers blood pressure and decreases Cortisol — a stress hormone — which can improve your mood.‌
Where to Practice Box Breathing
One benefit of box breathing is that you can practice it anywhere. You can practice it at work, at home, in public, or in a stressful situation. However, you don’t have to practice box breathing only when you are stressed. You can do it to calm your mind and body, allowing yourself to reset and stay fresh.

Tips for Box Breathing
If you're new to this breathing technique, here are some tips:

 If 4 seconds is too much, you can start with 2 or 3 seconds to help you start practicing.
Count the seconds in your head if it is hard to find the beat.
Ensure you are comfortable. It is easier to focus on your breath when you are relaxed and in a comfortable position

4-7-8 Relaxing Breath
What is 4-7-8 Breathing?

The 4-7-8 Relaxing Breath is a calming breathing technique popularized by Dr. Andrew Weil, inspired by traditional yogic breathing practices known as Pranayama. It follows a simple rhythm of inhaling, holding the breath, and exhaling slowly through the mouth. The extended exhale encourages the body to shift from a state of stress into one of deep relaxation.

Unlike energizing breathing methods, the 4-7-8 technique is designed to help quiet the mind, reduce physical tension, and prepare the body for rest.

How It Works

The technique emphasizes a long, controlled exhalation, which naturally activates the parasympathetic nervous system—often called the "rest and digest" system.

When the parasympathetic system becomes more active, your body may respond by:

Slowing the heart rate
Reducing muscle tension
Encouraging a feeling of calm
Lowering mental overactivity
Supporting the transition toward sleep

The breath hold also encourages slower, more mindful breathing and helps break the cycle of rapid, shallow breaths that often accompany stress.

Potential Benefits

People commonly practice the 4-7-8 technique to:

Relax before bedtime
Calm racing thoughts
Reduce everyday stress
Ease feelings of nervousness
Improve focus through slower breathing
Create a mindful pause during a busy day
Support relaxation after work or exercise

Many people notice a calming effect within just a few breathing cycles, although experiences vary from person to person.

Best Time to Practice

The 4-7-8 Relaxing Breath is especially useful when you want to slow down and unwind.

Common times include:

Before going to sleep
After a stressful meeting or exam
During periods of anxiety or overwhelm
Before meditation
During work breaks
While traveling
Before an important presentation
Anytime you need a moment to relax
Who Can Benefit?

This breathing exercise may be suitable for:

Students managing academic stress
Professionals with demanding schedules
People who struggle to relax before bed
Individuals practicing mindfulness
Anyone looking for a simple daily relaxation technique

No previous breathing experience is required.

The Science Behind It

Slow breathing has been widely studied for its effects on the autonomic nervous system.

Research suggests that slower breathing patterns may:

Increase parasympathetic activity
Improve heart rate variability (HRV)
Reduce perceived stress
Promote emotional regulation
Support relaxation and sleep quality

While the exact 4-7-8 pattern itself has limited direct clinical research, many of its principles—particularly slow breathing and extended exhalation—are supported by studies on controlled breathing techniques.

Tips for a Better Experience

For the best results:

Practice in a quiet, comfortable environment.
Keep your shoulders and jaw relaxed.
Breathe gently rather than forcefully.
Stay consistent by practicing daily.
Focus on making the exhale smooth and controlled.
If you feel lightheaded, return to normal breathing and try again later.
Safety

The 4-7-8 Relaxing Breath is generally considered a gentle breathing exercise for healthy adults.

However:

Practice while sitting or lying down when learning.
Stop if you feel dizzy or uncomfortable.
Do not practice while driving, swimming, or operating machinery.
If you have a significant heart condition, respiratory illness, or other medical concerns, consult a healthcare professional before beginning a new breathing practice.
Frequently Asked Questions
Is 4-7-8 breathing good for sleep?

Many people use it before bedtime because the slow breathing rhythm and extended exhale can promote relaxation and help quiet a busy mind.

How often should I practice?

Once or twice a day is a common starting point. Consistency is generally more beneficial than long sessions.

Is it normal to feel lightheaded?

Some beginners may feel slightly lightheaded when first practicing slow breathing. If this happens, stop, breathe normally, and try fewer cycles next time.

Is 4-7-8 breathing the same as box breathing?

No. Box breathing uses equal-length phases, while 4-7-8 places greater emphasis on a longer exhale, making it more focused on relaxation than concentration.

Can beginners try it?

Yes. It is one of the simplest breathing techniques to learn and is often recommended as an introduction to mindful breathing.

Summary

The 4-7-8 Relaxing Breath is a simple yet effective relaxation technique that combines slow inhalation, a comfortable breath hold, and an extended exhalation to encourage calmness. Whether you're winding down after a busy day, preparing for sleep, or simply taking a mindful break, it offers an easy way to slow your breathing and support your body's natural relaxation response.

Belly Breathing / Diaphragmatic Breathing
What is Belly Breathing?

Belly breathing, also called diaphragmatic breathing or abdominal breathing, is a breathing technique that emphasizes movement of the diaphragm and abdomen rather than excessive movement of the upper chest.

The diaphragm is the main muscle responsible for breathing. It is a dome-shaped muscle located beneath the lungs. When it contracts, it moves downward and creates space for the lungs to expand. When it relaxes, it moves upward as air leaves the lungs.

During diaphragmatic breathing, the abdomen gently rises during inhalation and falls during exhalation. This doesn't mean that air is actually entering the stomach—the movement of the belly happens because the diaphragm moves downward and the abdominal contents shift as the lungs expand.

Why Is It Called "Belly Breathing"?

The name comes from the visible movement of the abdomen.

When you breathe normally, your chest and abdomen can both move. Belly breathing simply encourages you to become more aware of the downward movement of the diaphragm and expansion of the abdomen.

It is therefore better understood as diaphragm-focused breathing rather than literally breathing into your belly.

How the Diaphragm Works

The diaphragm is the primary muscle of respiration.

During inhalation:

The diaphragm contracts.
It moves downward.
The space inside the chest increases.
Pressure inside the lungs decreases.
Air flows into the lungs.

During exhalation:

The diaphragm relaxes.
It moves upward.
The space inside the chest decreases.
Air flows out of the lungs.

The diaphragm performs most of the work of normal breathing, although other muscles can assist when breathing becomes more demanding.

Belly Breathing vs. Chest Breathing

These terms can sometimes be misleading.

Chest breathing generally describes breathing in which there is greater visible movement of the upper chest and shoulders.

Diaphragmatic breathing emphasizes movement generated by the diaphragm, often producing more noticeable abdominal movement.

Healthy breathing does not require eliminating chest movement completely. The goal is not to force the belly outward or keep the chest perfectly still. Instead, the practice encourages comfortable, efficient movement and relaxed breathing mechanics.

Potential Benefits

Diaphragmatic breathing has been studied for its effects on both physiological and psychological measures.

Potential benefits include:

Promoting relaxation
Reducing perceived stress
Supporting slower breathing
Improving awareness of breathing patterns
Supporting autonomic regulation
Producing short-term cardiovascular changes
Helping some people manage feelings of breathlessness
Supporting respiratory rehabilitation in certain clinical settings

A systematic review published in 2026 examined 48 randomized controlled trials and found evidence of potential benefits for several outcomes, including anxiety and acute cardiovascular measures in healthy adults. However, the researchers also emphasized substantial differences between studies and limitations in study quality.

Belly Breathing and Stress

One of the most common reasons people practice diaphragmatic breathing is stress management.

When people are stressed, breathing can become faster, shallower, or more irregular. Deliberately slowing the breath and relaxing the muscles around the chest, neck, and shoulders can provide a physical focus and encourage a calmer breathing pattern.

A systematic review found evidence that diaphragmatic breathing may reduce both physiological and psychological measures of stress, although the number of studies available at the time was small.

Effect on the Nervous System

Breathing is closely connected with the autonomic nervous system, which helps regulate functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, and digestion.

Slow, controlled diaphragmatic breathing can influence autonomic activity and cardiovascular regulation. Research reviews describe effects involving the respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and nervous systems.

This is one reason diaphragmatic breathing is often included in relaxation, mindfulness, rehabilitation, and stress-management practices.

Belly Breathing and Heart Rate

Breathing naturally affects heart rate.

Heart rate tends to increase somewhat during inhalation and decrease during exhalation. This normal relationship between breathing and heart rate is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia.

Slow breathing can make these natural fluctuations more pronounced and can influence measures of heart-rate variability.

However, a higher HRV reading should not automatically be interpreted as meaning that someone is healthier. HRV is affected by many factors, including age, fitness, sleep, stress, medications, and health conditions.

Belly Breathing and the Lungs

Diaphragmatic breathing is particularly relevant to respiratory rehabilitation.

The American Lung Association describes belly breathing as a technique that can help people with conditions such as COPD or asthma manage breathing and use less energy when breathing.

Research in COPD has also found that breathing exercises can improve certain measures of respiratory function and exercise capacity, although the quality and type of evidence varies between interventions.

It is important not to interpret this as meaning that diaphragmatic breathing can cure lung disease. It is better viewed as a supportive breathing technique that may be useful alongside appropriate medical care.

Belly Breathing and Anxiety

Slow breathing can provide a simple physical anchor when someone feels tense or overwhelmed.

By focusing attention on the movement of the abdomen and the rhythm of breathing, people may find it easier to shift attention away from racing thoughts.

Research has found potential reductions in anxiety with diaphragmatic breathing, although the exact effects vary between studies and individuals.

It should not, however, be presented as a replacement for professional treatment for an anxiety disorder.

Belly Breathing and Relaxation

Diaphragmatic breathing is often used as a relaxation technique because it combines:

Slow breathing
Reduced muscular tension
Attention to the breath
A steady respiratory rhythm
Greater awareness of physical sensations

The technique can therefore fit naturally into meditation, mindfulness, yoga, relaxation routines, and breathing exercises.

Belly Breathing and Sleep

Some people use diaphragmatic breathing as part of a bedtime relaxation routine.

Its potential value for sleep is likely related to its ability to encourage slower breathing and relaxation rather than any special sleep-inducing mechanism.

If someone has persistent insomnia or another sleep disorder, breathing exercises should not replace appropriate medical evaluation.

Belly Breathing and Exercise

The diaphragm is heavily involved in normal breathing during physical activity.

Diaphragmatic breathing exercises can also be used in respiratory rehabilitation and physical therapy. Research has found potential improvements in exercise-related outcomes in some populations, including people with respiratory conditions.

However, during intense exercise, breathing naturally becomes deeper and faster. Trying to force an unusually slow breathing pattern during strenuous exercise is not the purpose of this technique.

Belly Breathing and Digestive Health

The diaphragm and abdominal region are mechanically connected, so breathing can influence pressure within the abdomen.

Diaphragmatic breathing has also been investigated as a complementary approach for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis found a modest improvement in GERD symptom scores, although the studies were heterogeneous and the authors emphasized that the evidence remains limited.

Therefore, it is more accurate to say that diaphragmatic breathing may support some people with digestive symptoms rather than claiming that it treats or cures reflux.

When People Use Belly Breathing

Common situations include:

During stressful moments
Before meditation
During mindfulness practice
Before bedtime
During relaxation breaks
As part of yoga
During respiratory rehabilitation
When learning to control breathing patterns
Before or after a demanding activity

The American Lung Association recommends learning and practicing the technique when breathing comfortably rather than waiting until significant shortness of breath occurs.

What Should Belly Breathing Feel Like?

A comfortable practice should generally feel:

Gentle
Smooth
Relaxed
Unforced
Rhythmic

The abdomen may rise during inhalation and fall during exhalation.

Your shoulders and neck should not need to work excessively.

There is no need to take the biggest breath possible. Overly deep or forceful breathing can sometimes cause lightheadedness or discomfort.

Common Mistakes
Forcing the belly outward

Belly breathing isn't about pushing your stomach outward as far as possible.

The movement should come naturally from the diaphragm and abdominal region.

Taking extremely large breaths

More air isn't automatically better.

Very deep or rapid breathing can change carbon dioxide levels and may cause dizziness or tingling.

Raising the shoulders

If the shoulders rise dramatically with every breath, you may be relying heavily on accessory breathing muscles rather than allowing the diaphragm to work comfortably.

Trying to eliminate all chest movement

The chest naturally moves during breathing. Diaphragmatic breathing does not require keeping the chest completely motionless.

Breathing too quickly

The purpose is generally to create a comfortable and controlled breathing pattern, not to maximize the amount of air moved.

Belly Breathing vs. Pursed-Lip Breathing

These are related but different techniques.

Diaphragmatic breathing focuses primarily on using the diaphragm and creating relaxed abdominal movement.

Pursed-lip breathing involves breathing out through partially closed lips, creating gentle resistance during exhalation.

They are sometimes combined in respiratory rehabilitation, particularly for people with COPD.

Belly Breathing vs. 4-7-8 Breathing

The two techniques have different goals.

Diaphragmatic breathing emphasizes relaxed breathing mechanics and awareness of the diaphragm.

4-7-8 breathing uses a specific timing pattern involving inhalation, breath retention, and a prolonged exhalation.

A person can perform 4-7-8 breathing with diaphragmatic breathing, but the techniques themselves are not identical.

Who Can Practice It?

For many healthy adults, diaphragmatic breathing is a simple and accessible breathing exercise.

It is also used by healthcare and rehabilitation professionals in certain settings, including pulmonary rehabilitation.

However, people with significant respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological, or other medical conditions should discuss new breathing exercises with an appropriate healthcare professional when there is uncertainty about safety.

Safety

Belly breathing should feel comfortable rather than forced.

Stop the exercise and return to normal breathing if you experience:

Significant dizziness
Lightheadedness
Chest pain
Unusual or worsening shortness of breath
Faintness
Any other concerning symptoms

Do not practice breathing exercises while driving, swimming, or operating machinery if the exercise could cause dizziness or impair your ability to respond safely.

If someone is experiencing severe or unexplained difficulty breathing, a breathing exercise should not be used as a substitute for urgent medical care.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is belly breathing the same as diaphragmatic breathing?

Yes. Belly breathing is a common name for diaphragmatic breathing because the abdomen often rises and falls noticeably as the diaphragm moves.

Are you actually breathing into your stomach?

No. Air enters the lungs, not the stomach. The term "belly breathing" describes the movement of the abdomen caused by the diaphragm's movement.

Is belly breathing better than chest breathing?

Not necessarily. Both chest and abdominal movement are normal parts of breathing. Diaphragmatic breathing is a technique used to encourage relaxed, efficient breathing rather than a claim that chest movement is inherently bad.

Can belly breathing reduce stress?

It may. Research suggests that diaphragmatic breathing can reduce some physiological and psychological measures of stress, although individual responses vary.

Can belly breathing help with shortness of breath?

It can help some people manage breathing and reduce the effort associated with breathing, particularly in respiratory rehabilitation. The American Lung Association specifically describes its use for people with COPD and asthma.

However, new, severe, or persistent shortness of breath needs appropriate medical evaluation.

Can belly breathing improve lung capacity?

It is better not to promise that it increases lung capacity. Diaphragmatic breathing can influence breathing mechanics and respiratory function, but effects vary depending on the person and underlying condition.

Can I practice belly breathing every day?

For many people, it can be incorporated into a daily relaxation or breathing routine. The important thing is to keep the breathing comfortable and avoid forcing excessively deep breaths.

Summary

Belly breathing, or diaphragmatic breathing, is a simple technique that emphasizes relaxed movement of the diaphragm and abdomen during breathing.

Because the diaphragm is the body's primary breathing muscle, learning to use it comfortably can improve awareness of breathing mechanics and may support relaxation, stress management, and respiratory function.

Research suggests potential benefits across several areas, including stress, anxiety, cardiovascular regulation, and certain respiratory or digestive conditions, but the evidence is not equally strong for every claimed benefit.

The most important principle is simple:

Breathe comfortably, gently, and without forcing the breath.
